Emotion in the Voice

What actors put into their voice, what listeners actually hear, and which acoustic cues carry the difference. The groundwork for a small, expressive Whissle TTS that is trained on perceived emotion, not acted labels.

measures

Each emotion has its own acoustic fingerprint

Every emotional clip is compared with the same actor reading the same sentence neutrally (the parallel-reference method of Bou-Ghazale & Hansen, 1995). Cells show the effect size of that difference: mean change divided by its spread.

Angry is loud with a flat spectral tilt and a raised first formant (the mouth opens wider). Fear stands out on CPPS, an irregular, less periodic voice. Sad is slow, quiet and breathy (H1–H2 up). Hover a cell for the raw change in its own units.

convinces

What makes a listener believe it

Within each emotion, a logistic model predicts whether at least half the raters heard the intended emotion from the twelve parallel cues (actor-disjoint cross-validation). Bars are standardised weights: right means more of this cue makes the emotion more convincing.

Happy is sold by pitch range, not pitch level: the contour shape matters. Anger is sold by loudness. For sad, none of these cues predicts belief (AUC 0.50), so it needs stronger cues in the data we record.

machine

Models hear more than people do

Six-way recognition of the acted emotion on actors never seen in training (unweighted average recall, chance 0.167). Actors leave consistent traces that listeners often miss, so a TTS must exaggerate cues past "acted" to be heard.

Recognition by method

Speaker normalisation is worth +0.09 on its own. WavLM probes subtract each actor's mean embedding, which uses that actor's other clips, so read its score as an upper bound.

WavLM-base+ by layer

Emotion peaks in the middle layers (5–7). Crowd-agreed clips are easier. Sentence identity is near-perfect from layer 2, so the representation carries content too.

Model budget

Non-autoregressive, single pass, chunk-streamable. Context lives outside the budget: Whissle's speech understanding (emotion and intent of the user's turn) and the reply planner emit the style plan, a fixed tag set of emotion soft-vector, intensity and per-word emphasis. That fixed tag set is the controlled vocabulary.

Training signals

  1. Spectrogram and adversarial losses for the decoder and vocoder.
  2. Per-word prosody targets for the planner, phone-level targets for the variance adaptor.
  3. A heard-as-intended loss: a frozen mel-domain copy of the Whissle ear scores the generated speech against the requested emotion and intensity.
  4. Intensity calibrated so 1.0 matches the best real takes; Bou-Ghazale's perturbed speech overshot real speech (92% vs 85% heard as loud).

How we will judge it

  1. Heard-as-intended rate from the Whissle ear and a listener panel.
  2. Pairwise listening test: which is more angry, both, or neither.
  3. Intensity monotonicity: 0.3 → 0.6 → 0.9 must rank correctly.
  4. Intelligibility (Whissle ASR WER) and naturalness at the neutral baseline; CPU real-time factor.

Next steps, in order

  1. Phone-level forced alignment (MFA) for CREMA-D and ESD; the CTC word timings used here are too coarse for duration work.
  2. Train the Whissle ear on WavLM layer 6: crowd soft emotion, perceived intensity, arousal and dominance.
  3. Annotate ESD and the LibriTTS-R subset with the schema above.
  4. Train the 9.6M acoustic model and vocoder; ablate the heard-as-intended loss.
  5. Write the Whissle recording script from these findings and record the production voice.
